infrastructure for the empirical asset pricing data workflow. This

\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

Factor returns enter empirical asset pricing tests, optimal portfolio
allocation, and risk-management decisions. Constructing them typically
involves a three-stage pipeline: data acquisition, usually downloading
and cleaning raw return and accounting data from the Center for Research
in Security Prices (CRSP) and Compustat; variable construction,
including appropriate lags to avoid look-ahead bias; and portfolio
sorting, where securities are assigned to portfolios based on
characteristics. Each stage involves consequential design choices such
as how to handle delistings, which fiscal-year lag to apply, where to
set breakpoints, and whether portfolios should be equal- or
value-weighted. Few pipelines are standardized, and fewer still ship
with executable code. Therefore, reproducing or extending a factor
series often starts with reconstructing the pipeline from scratch, with
no way to verify that the result matches the original approach.

No widely adopted tool covers this upstream workflow. The most popular
factor sources publish \emph{series} without the code that produced
them: Ken French's Data Library \citep{fama.2023} supplies the
Fama-French three- and five-factor returns and dozens of sorted
portfolios. Some other projects provide code: the Open Source Bond Asset
Pricing project \citep{dickerson.2023}, available at
\href{https://openbondassetpricing.com/}{openbondassetpricing.com},
covers fixed income; the Open Source Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing
repository \citep{chen.2022} reproduces 213+ published anomalies, and
\citet{jensen.2023} provide a global factor library spanning hundreds of
long-short signals across many countries. None of these sources,
however, exposes the preprocessing pipeline as configurable code that
researchers can modify. Many individual authors also host their own
published factors, with transparency about construction choices varying
widely. \citet{walter.2024} and \citet{soebhag.2024} show that these
choices significantly affect the outcome of portfolio sorts. Existing
\proglang{R} packages for quantitative finance operate downstream of
factor construction, i.e., on price and return panels that the user must
already have. Between raw data and standard asset-pricing tests sits an
opaque layer, where construction choices accumulate silently.

This paper introduces \pkg{tidyfinance}, an \proglang{R} package on CRAN
that exposes each stage of the pipeline as configurable code. The
package addresses each stage with a dedicated interface. A unified
\texttt{download\_data()} function cleans, aligns, and harmonizes panels
from Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS), Ken French's Data Library,
Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED), and seven other providers,
including current constituents for 20 major equity indexes;
Table~\ref{tbl-data-sources} catalogs the full set. The WRDS panels
cover CRSP, Compustat, the Trade Reporting and Compliance Engine
(TRACE), and the Mergent Fixed Income Securities Database (FISD).
Composable helpers then automate fiscal-year lagging, breakpoint
computation, and portfolio aggregation. Every construction choice is
exposed as a function argument with a default that follows established
convention (for instance, value-weights and the Fama-French fiscal-year
lag). A default call reproduces standard practice; overriding any
default takes one argument.

We illustrate the package by reproducing a version of the asset growth
factor of \citet{cooper.2008} end-to-end from raw CRSP and Compustat
data, then varying any construction choice with a single-argument
change. To spare researchers the work of rebuilding the pipeline, we run
\pkg{tidyfinance} across many defensible combinations and publish the
results as a factor library of 50 sorting variables across 841,536
specifications. The library is available through a single
\texttt{download\_data()} call or an interactive dashboard at
\href{https://factors.tidy-finance.org}{factors.tidy-finance.org}. The
package operationalizes the workflow developed in the open-access
textbooks \emph{Tidy Finance with \proglang{R}} \citep{scheuch.2023} and
\emph{Tidy Finance with \proglang{Python}} \citep{scheuch.2024}, which
present each step in tutorial form with inline scripts. The contribution
of this paper is \pkg{tidyfinance} itself: a unified, configurable,
tested, and versioned \proglang{R} package for this workflow. Where the
books teach a reader how to assemble the pipeline from scratch, the
package supplies it as a single dependency that researchers can call,
audit, and reuse without rebuilding the underlying code.

\section{Background and related work}\label{sec-background}

The \proglang{R} ecosystem for empirical asset pricing splits cleanly
into three groups. Each addresses one slice of the workflow and leaves
the rest to the user.

\textbf{Data-acquisition packages.} Several \proglang{R} packages
retrieve specific data sources commonly used in asset pricing.
\pkg{frenchdata} \citep{frenchdata.2021} downloads and parses the zip
archives from Ken French's Data Library into long data frames; it does
not touch WRDS and does not implement any construction step beyond
reading the published factor series. \pkg{quantmod} \citep{ryan.2025}
and \pkg{tidyquant} \citep{dancho.2026} cover Yahoo Finance, FRED, and
Tiingo, returning daily open, high, low, and close price series and a
small set of derived return helpers; neither connects to WRDS, and
neither covers factor-construction logic. \pkg{Rblpapi}
\citep{armstrong.Rblpapi} queries the Bloomberg terminal for
subscribers; the returned series leave the same factor-construction gap
unfilled as the packages above. None of these packages covers the
WRDS-CRSP-Compustat panel or the cleaning, alignment, and survivorship
conventions the asset-pricing literature requires.

\textbf{Downstream analysis packages.} Once a cleaned panel is in hand,
several packages support analysis. \pkg{PerformanceAnalytics}
\citep{peterson.2026} computes performance and risk metrics for return
series. \pkg{fPortfolio} \citep{wuertz.fPortfolio} covers portfolio
optimization. \pkg{RQuantLib} \citep{eddelbuettel.RQuantLib} interfaces
with QuantLib for derivative pricing. \pkg{highfrequency}
\citep{boudt.highfrequency} cleans intraday trade-and-quote panels and
implements microstructure-volatility estimators. Each of these tools
assumes that the panel of returns and factors is already constructed;
none implements a factor-construction layer.

\textbf{Factor data releases.} A group of projects publishes
\emph{factor return series} directly. Ken French's Data Library
\citep{fama.2023} supplies the canonical \citet{fama.1993} three- and
five-factor returns and dozens of sorted portfolios, but documents
construction in prose only and provide no associated code.
\citet{akey.2026} show that these series change retroactively as the
underlying method is silently revised, so that the factor returns a
researcher obtains depend on \emph{when} they were downloaded, with
material effects on mutual-fund performance and cross-sectional asset
pricing tests. A transparent, code-first pipeline removes precisely this
opacity. Three more recent projects pair the published factor data with
the construction script that produced it. \citet{hou.2020} replicate 452
cross-sectional anomalies and document that most fail out-of-sample,
attributing the failures in part to undocumented construction variation
across studies. The Open Source Cross-Sectional Asset Pricing repository
\citep{chen.2022} responds by publishing per-anomaly construction
scripts alongside the resulting 213+ long-short returns. The Open Source
Bond Asset Pricing project \citep{dickerson.2023} does the same for
corporate bond factors. \citet{jensen.2023} provide the Global Factor
Data of hundreds of long-short signals across many countries, together
with the construction code in \proglang{Python}. These projects
represent a substantial move toward transparency: a reader can inspect
exactly how each factor was built and reproduce its returns. Two
limitations remain. First, none exposes the pipeline as a callable API.
A user who wants the asset growth factor rebalanced in January instead
of July, or formed on NYSE-plus-NASDAQ breakpoints, or computed on a
different fiscal-year lag, must fork the published script and edit it by
hand. The script is not a function with named arguments. Second, the
factor returns are frozen at the release date, with no in-workflow path
to refresh them as new observations arrive or as a researcher's design
choices evolve.

\textbf{\pkg{tidyfinance} fills the missing gap:} the package supplies
the cleaned WRDS-CRSP-Compustat panel that data-acquisition packages do
not produce and exposes the factor-construction pipeline that downstream
analysis packages assume to be in hand. One \texttt{download\_data()}
call returns a cleaned WRDS-CRSP-Compustat panel; further functions
implement the full factor-construction pipeline (fiscal-year lagging,
breakpoint computation, portfolio sorting, long-short aggregation) as a
chain of composable steps whose arguments cover the construction choices
that vary across studies in the literature above. Two design principles,
both inherited from \citet{scheuch.2023} and \citet{scheuch.2024}, guide
the API. First, each construction decision is \emph{transparent}: it
appears as a configurable parameter with a documented default, in line
with the tidy tools principles of \citet{wickham.2023}. Second, the
pipeline is \emph{modular} by construction: each construction step is
implemented as a narrowly scoped function with clearly defined inputs
and outputs. Researchers can therefore either chain these functions into
a complete factor-construction workflow or reuse individual components
within their own pipelines. \pkg{tidyfinance} additionally covers asset
pricing tests and optimal portfolio choice; this paper focuses on factor
construction and defers the remaining functionality to dedicated
treatments.

\section{Package overview}\label{sec-overview}

\subsection{Installation and setup}\label{sec-installation}

You can install the released version of \pkg{tidyfinance}
\href{https://cran.r-project.org/package=tidyfinance}{from CRAN} via:

\begin{verbatim}
R> install.packages("tidyfinance")
\end{verbatim}

To install the development version of \pkg{tidyfinance} from
\href{https://github.com/tidy-finance/r-tidyfinance}{GitHub}, first
install \pkg{pak}, then run:

\begin{verbatim}
R> install.packages("pak")
R> pak::pak("tidy-finance/r-tidyfinance")
\end{verbatim}

Once installed, load the package with:

\begin{verbatim}
R> library("tidyfinance")
\end{verbatim}

acquisition, variable construction, and portfolio sorting. Each stage
involves dozens of small decisions: sample filters, cleaning rules,
rebalancing conventions, and breakpoint definitions.

\pkg{tidyfinance}, grouped by workflow stage. The remainder of this
section walks through them in narrative order.

For empirical asset pricing work, WRDS is the common source of return
and accounting panels. WRDS data are proprietary and require an
institutional subscription and credentials. \pkg{tidyfinance} handles

the connection proceeds normally.

\texttt{download\_data()} applies standard cleaning conventions
following \citet{bali.2016} and \citet{scheuch.2023} automatically. For
CRSP monthly data, the function restricts the universe to common equity
securities listed on NYSE, AMEX (now NYSE American), or NASDAQ. It
computes market capitalization as shares outstanding times price,
constructs a one-month market capitalization lag for portfolio weights,
and maps listing exchange codes and Standard Industrial Classification
(SIC) codes to readable labels. Excess returns are raw returns minus a
risk-free rate that \pkg{tidyfinance} constructs from FRED by splicing
the 3-month Treasury bill secondary-market rate (pre-2001) with the
4-week Treasury bill secondary-market rate (from 2001 onward); the
spliced series is hosted on Hugging Face and refreshed monthly by a
scheduled GitHub Actions workflow. The function drops observations with
missing returns or market capitalization, and incorporates delisting
returns directly, eliminating the need for a separate adjustment. The
function defaults to the CRSP 2.0 (CIZ) layout first introduced in July
2022. Alternatively, passing \texttt{version\ =\ "v1"} returns the
legacy (SIZ) layout, which ends in December 2024. \citet{schwarz.2026}
document that the move to CIZ rewrites a non-trivial share of historical
monthly returns through a changed dividend reinvestment assumption, yet
find that average premia and their significance are largely unaffected.

For Compustat annual data, \texttt{download\_data()} restricts to
industrial-format consolidated statements and retains only the most
recent observation per firm-year. Following \citet{fama.1992}, it
constructs book equity in priority order: stockholders' equity when
available, else common equity plus preferred stock, else total assets
minus total liabilities. The function adds back deferred taxes and
investment tax credits, and subtracts the book value of preferred stock.

For TRACE corporate bond transactions, \texttt{download\_data()} applies
the Dick-Nielsen cleaning protocol \citep{dicknielsen.2009} to remove
cancellations, corrections, and reversals before returning the trade
panel.

Across \texttt{download\_data()}, every returned panel exposes a single
\texttt{date} column of \texttt{\textless{}date\textgreater{}} type,
regardless of source. Joining observations from different providers
therefore reduces to an equality join on \texttt{date} without manual
format conversion. The convention is the same across frequencies: daily
observations return the trading date, monthly observations the first
calendar day of the reference month (regardless of whether it was a
trading day), and quarterly and annual observations the first day of the
fiscal period-end month as reported by the original source. For
Compustat, the latter is the firm's fiscal period-end
(\texttt{datadate}). Macroeconomic series from FRED keep their original
release dates. Where the underlying source uses an internal date that
differs from this convention, \pkg{tidyfinance} preserves it as an
auxiliary column: CRSP 2.0 monthly returns, for example, retain the
trading-date \texttt{mthcaldt} as \texttt{calculation\_date} alongside
the harmonized \texttt{date}. Cross-frequency joins, such as merging
monthly returns with annual fundamentals, require the lagging logic
described in Section~\ref{sec-variable-construction}.

\subsection{Variable construction}\label{sec-variable-construction}

Most predictive variables in asset pricing studies use lagged
information so that, for example, portfolio formation in month \(t\)
uses only data observable at the end of month \(t-1\).
\texttt{add\_lagged\_columns()} automates the required lagging
procedure. For each row in the data, it looks back over a configurable
window and attaches the most recent observed value of the chosen
columns. Figure~\ref{fig-lagging-illustration} illustrates the two modes
the function supports. By default (\texttt{ff\_adjustment\ =\ FALSE}),
shown in Panel A, the window is bounded by \texttt{lag} (the minimum
delay between observation and use) and \texttt{max\_lag} (the maximum
delay before a stale value is treated as missing). Within this window
(the dashed rectangle), the function picks the most recent non-missing
value; if no observation falls in the window, the lagged column takes
\texttt{NA}.

The second mode, enabled by \texttt{ff\_adjustment\ =\ TRUE} and shown
in Panel B of Figure~\ref{fig-lagging-illustration}, implements the
Fama-French convention \citep{fama.1993}. The convention assumes
accounting variables for fiscal year \(t-1\) become public information
by June of year \(t\), then uses them for portfolio formation from July
of \(t\) through June of \(t+1\). This timing produces a step-function
lag that updates once per year at the June/July boundary, regardless of
the underlying fiscal year-end: all months from July through June share
the value locked in at the previous rebalancing.

\texttt{join\_lagged\_values()} is a related helper that merges lagged
values from one data frame into another using the same windowing logic.
It is useful when the target panel and the source panel are at different
frequencies, for example, when joining monthly accounting data into a
quarterly target panel. The function takes the same
\texttt{ff\_adjustment} argument as \texttt{add\_lagged\_columns()}, so
the Fama-French convention can be applied at the join step rather than
as a separate transformation.

}

\caption{\label{fig-lagging-illustration}Two lagging mechanisms in
\texttt{add\_lagged\_columns()}. Each row represents a data series with
observed (filled) and missing (hollow) cells. The dashed rectangle marks
the search window from the perspective of the most recent observation
(the top row); arrows show which value gets matched to each target

A handful of additional helpers cover routine operations that recur
across empirical studies. \texttt{winsorize()} and \texttt{trim()}
handle outliers in cross-sectional variables: the former symmetrically
replaces values below the \(q\)-th and above the \((1-q)\)-th percentile
with the corresponding quantile values, while the latter sets them to
\texttt{NA}. \texttt{estimate\_model()} fits a linear model from a
formula string and returns the coefficients as a tidy one-row data
frame, which composes naturally with \texttt{group\_by()} for grouped or
rolling regressions such as estimating time-series factor exposures
stock by stock via \texttt{estimate\_betas()}.
\texttt{compute\_rolling\_value()} applies an arbitrary aggregation
function over a rolling window defined in calendar time rather than row
counts, accommodating irregular observation frequencies and missing
periods. \texttt{filter\_sorting\_data()} screens a panel before
sorting, dropping observations that fail the sample filters bundled in a
\texttt{filter\_options()} object, such as financial or utility firms or
stocks below a minimum price or size; by default it reports how many
observations each filter removes, which \texttt{quiet\ =\ TRUE}
suppresses.

\subsection{Portfolio sorting}\label{sec-portfolio-sorting}

A portfolio sort groups stocks each period into ordered portfolios
according to the level of a sorting variable. The boundaries between
portfolios are called \emph{breakpoints}: numeric cutoffs of the sorting
variable that determine which portfolio a stock joins. A quintile sort
on size, for instance, has four breakpoints at the 20th, 40th, 60th, and
80th percentiles of the cross-sectional market-capitalization
distribution. Common sorting variables include size (measured as market
capitalization), beta, book-to-market, and momentum. The long-short
return between the extreme portfolios is interpreted as a
factor-mimicking strategy and serves as the basis for tests of whether
the sorting variable forecasts future returns. Portfolio sorts are among
the most widely used tools in empirical asset pricing \citep{bali.2016}.
We expose the procedure through four composable building blocks plus a
one-call wrapper.

\texttt{compute\_breakpoints()} takes a cross-section and a sorting
variable and returns the numeric cutoffs that define the portfolio
boundaries. The user supplies either a number of portfolios (for
example, five for quintiles) or an explicit vector of percentiles, but
not both. A \texttt{breakpoints\_exchanges} argument restricts the
universe used to compute breakpoints. The standard convention is
NYSE-listed stocks only, which prevents the large cross-section of small
NASDAQ-listed firms from dominating the breakpoints \citep{jensen.2023}.
A related \texttt{breakpoints\_min\_size\_threshold} argument excludes
stocks below a market-cap quantile from the breakpoint computation,
which size-related sorts often require because microcaps would otherwise
distort the cutoffs. A \texttt{smooth\_bunching} flag handles cases
where the sorting variable takes the same value for many observations at
the bottom or top of the distribution, so that the lowest (or highest)
quantile breakpoints coincide. Without intervention, all tied
observations would land in a single boundary portfolio while neighboring
portfolios remain empty. When \texttt{smooth\_bunching\ =\ TRUE}, the
function assigns the bunched observations to the boundary portfolio and
recomputes the remaining breakpoints on the subset of observations
strictly inside the boundary value, restoring well-defined cutoffs for
the interior portfolios.

\texttt{assign\_portfolio()} maps each observation to a portfolio
integer and uses breakpoints supplied either directly or via a
breakpoint-computing function (defaulting to
\texttt{compute\_breakpoints()}). In practice,
\texttt{assign\_portfolio()} is called separately for each rebalancing
date, so that portfolios are reformed each period rather than fixed once
for the whole sample.

\texttt{compute\_portfolio\_returns()} ties the lower-level pieces
together. Given a panel of stock data, one or two sorting variables, a
sorting method, and \texttt{breakpoint\_options}, it handles
rebalancing, breakpoint computation, portfolio assignment, and return
aggregation. It then returns a complete panel of portfolio returns. For
each portfolio-date the function reports three return measures:
equal-weighted, value-weighted using \texttt{mktcap\_lag} as the weight,
and capped value-weighted \citep{jensen.2023}. The capped version pulls
weights above the \texttt{cap\_weight} percentile (default \texttt{0.8}
corresponds to \(80\%\)) back to that cap, which prevents a few megacap
firms from dominating the portfolio. A \texttt{min\_portfolio\_size}
argument sets the minimum number of constituents below which the
corresponding return is set to \texttt{NA}. An optional
\texttt{rebalancing\_month} argument switches from period-by-period
rebalancing to annual rebalancing in the specified month. Portfolios
formed in that month are then held constant for the following twelve
months.

The function supports three sorting schemes, illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig-illustrate-portfolio-helper}. A \emph{univariate} sort
uses a single variable. An \emph{independent bivariate} sort computes
breakpoints on each variable separately and intersects the resulting
bins to produce a two-way grid. A \emph{dependent bivariate} sort first
partitions the cross-section by the secondary (control) variable and
then re-sorts within each bucket on the main variable, which is the
appropriate choice when the second variable is treated as a control
rather than as an independent dimension of variation. For bivariate
sorts, returns are averaged over the secondary variable so that only the
main-variable portfolios are returned.

\begin{figure}[H]

\centering{

\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth,height=\textheight,keepaspectratio]{jss-manuscript_files/figure-pdf/fig-illustrate-portfolio-helper-1.pdf}

}

\caption{\label{fig-illustrate-portfolio-helper}Portfolio sort
approaches illustrated on a simulated cross-section. Each panel plots

\texttt{compute\_long\_short\_returns()} takes the output of
\texttt{compute\_portfolio\_returns()} and computes the spread between
the top and bottom portfolios. A \texttt{direction} argument controls
the sign and defaults to \texttt{"top\_minus\_bottom"}. The function
returns one long-short series per return measure available in the input,
so the equal-weighted, value-weighted, and capped value-weighted spreads
are produced together. This long-short return is the factor-mimicking
spread that the asset-pricing literature typically takes as the object
of interest.

For users who want the full pipeline in a single call,
\texttt{implement\_portfolio\_sort()} wraps the four functions above. It
takes a panel of stock data, one or two sorting variables, a sorting
method, and a configuration object, and returns the long-short factor
return series alongside the underlying portfolio returns. The
lower-level functions remain available for custom workflows that need
finer control over breakpoints, weighting, or rebalancing.

\subsection{Variable construction}\label{sec-data-cleaning}

We illustrate data cleaning and variable construction using asset growth
(AG) as our running example. Asset growth for a firm \(i\) in year \(t\)
is the year-over-year percentage change in total assets
\citep{cooper.2008},

\[
AG_{t,i} = (AT_{t,i} - AT_{t-1,i}) / AT_{t-1,i}\text{,}
\]

where \(AT_{t,i}\) is total assets for firm \(i\) in year \(t\)
(retrieved in column \texttt{at}).

The \texttt{add\_lagged\_columns()} function from \pkg{tidyfinance}
handles temporal alignment by creating a lagged version of total assets
grouped by firm identifier (\texttt{gvkey}) with a one-year lag.
Non-finite values (arising when lagged total assets are zero or missing)
become \texttt{NA}.

\begin{verbatim}
R> sorting_variable_data <- compustat_annual |>
+  add_lagged_columns(
+    cols = "at",
+    lag = years(1),
+    by = "gvkey"
+  ) |>
+  mutate(
+    asset_growth = (at - at_lag) / at_lag,
+    asset_growth = if_else(is.finite(asset_growth), asset_growth, NA_real_)
+  ) |>
+  select(gvkey, date, asset_growth, ib)
\end{verbatim}

The call relies on two silent defaults: \texttt{max\_lag} falls back to
\texttt{lag} (here one year), so only the single observation exactly one
year before each row is eligible; \texttt{ff\_adjustment\ =\ FALSE}, so
the look-back is the plain calendar window rather than the Fama-French
June/July rebalancing convention introduced in
Section~\ref{sec-variable-construction}.

\texttt{sorting\_variable\_data} contains one observation per firm-year,
the natural unit for cross-sectional analysis of annual accounting
variables. Asset growth is heavily right-skewed: a small number of firms

\subsection{Portfolio sorts}\label{sec-portfolio-sorts}

In portfolio sorts, stocks are ranked each period by their sorting
variable and divided into quantile portfolios. Portfolio returns are
equal-weighted or value-weighted averages of constituent stock returns.
The long-short factor return is the difference between the extreme
portfolios. For asset growth, the relevant spread is bottom minus top,
reflecting the negative premium documented by \citet{cooper.2008}. The
construction involves several methodological choices: which stocks enter
the universe, how many portfolios to form, which exchange determines the
breakpoints, the weighting scheme, and the rebalancing frequency.

\texttt{implement\_portfolio\_sort()} exposes all of these choices
through a single function call. The function takes the panel data, one
or two sorting variables, a sorting method, and a
\texttt{portfolio\_sort\_options} object that bundles two configuration
layers: \texttt{filter\_options()} controls which observations enter the
breakpoint and return computation (for example, excluding financial
firms or stocks below a minimum price), and
\texttt{breakpoint\_options()} controls how portfolios are formed
(number of portfolios, exchange universe, bunching). Internally, the
function applies the sample filters and then calls
\texttt{compute\_portfolio\_returns()}, which produces the time series
of portfolio returns. A subsequent call to
\texttt{compute\_long\_short\_returns()} constructs the long-short
portfolio.

\texttt{compute\_portfolio\_returns()} itself rests on two lower-level
helpers, \texttt{compute\_breakpoints()} for quantile cutoffs and
\texttt{assign\_portfolio()} for mapping each stock-month to a portfolio
number. Both are exported, so researchers who need finer control can
call them directly rather than through the wrapper. The two examples
below use \texttt{implement\_portfolio\_sort()} throughout.

\subsubsection{Baseline specification (univariate
sorts)}\label{sec-baseline-sorts}

The simplest call to \texttt{implement\_portfolio\_sort()} requires only
a \texttt{portfolio\_sort\_options} object that specifies how to form
the breakpoints. In a univariate sort, stocks are ranked on a single
characteristic. We illustrate using asset growth as the sorting
variable, forming quintile portfolios with breakpoints computed from
NYSE-listed stocks only, and rebalancing annually in July.

\begin{verbatim}
R> options_baseline <- portfolio_sort_options(
+  breakpoint_options_main = breakpoint_options(
+    n_portfolios = 5,
+    breakpoints_exchanges = "NYSE"
+  )
+)
\end{verbatim}

The \texttt{breakpoint\_options()} call specifies that breakpoints come
only from NYSE-listed stocks, a common convention that avoids the
influence of small AMEX and NASDAQ firms on portfolio cutoffs. We then
pass these options to \texttt{implement\_portfolio\_sort()}.

\begin{verbatim}
R> portfolio_returns <- implement_portfolio_sort(
+  data = sorting_data,
+  sorting_variables = "asset_growth",
+  sorting_method = "univariate",
+  rebalancing_month = 7,
+  portfolio_sort_options = options_baseline
+)
\end{verbatim}

Apart from setting \texttt{rebalancing\_month\ =\ 7}, every other choice
point falls back to the package default: a cap-weight cutoff at the 80th
percentile of market capitalization for the capped value-weighted
return, no minimum portfolio size, and no smooth bunching. The standard
CRSP/Compustat column names (\texttt{permno}, \texttt{date},
\texttt{ret\_excess}, \texttt{mktcap\_lag}, \texttt{exchange}) are
picked up automatically through the default \texttt{data\_options()}.
Setting \texttt{rebalancing\_month\ =\ 7} reforms the portfolios each
July and holds them for the following twelve months, in line with the
standard Fama-French rebalancing convention. This schedule pairs with
\texttt{ff\_adjustment\ =\ TRUE} in \texttt{join\_lagged\_values()}: the
signal is locked in at the June/July boundary, so July is the first
month in which a fresh fiscal-year update is available.

\texttt{compute\_portfolio\_returns()}, called internally, returns a
long data frame with one row per portfolio-month and the three weighting
columns introduced in Section~\ref{sec-portfolio-sorting}:
\texttt{ret\_excess\_vw} (value-weighted, VW), \texttt{ret\_excess\_ew}
(equal-weighted, EW), and \texttt{ret\_excess\_vw\_capped} (capped
value-weighted, VWC). Piping the result through
\texttt{compute\_long\_short\_returns()} gives the long-short portfolio.
The \texttt{direction\ =\ "bottom\_minus\_top"} argument means the
factor is long low-AG stocks and short high-AG stocks.

\begin{verbatim}
R> factor_returns_ag <- portfolio_returns |>

\subsubsection{Customized specification (bivariate
sorts)}\label{sec-customized-sorts}

The baseline specification above takes nearly every package default and
runs a univariate sort to form long-short portfolios. The customized
specification below changes both: it overrides several of those defaults
through \texttt{portfolio\_sort\_options} and moves from sorting on one
variable to sorting on two.

We adopt a specification that combines, in a single call to
\texttt{implement\_portfolio\_sort()}, the universe restrictions and
breakpoint conventions used in many empirical studies: exclude
financials and utilities, exclude firms with non-positive earnings,
ignore the smallest 20\% of NYSE-listed firms when computing
breakpoints, form quintiles on AG within size buckets, use NYSE-only
breakpoints, rebalance annually in July, and require at least ten
constituents per portfolio.

Bivariate sorts control for a second variable, typically firm size
(\texttt{mktcap\_lag}), when forming portfolios on the main
characteristic. We use the dependent variant introduced in
Section~\ref{sec-portfolio-sorting} and illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig-illustrate-portfolio-helper}: stocks are first split
into size buckets, then sorted into asset growth portfolios within each
bucket, with returns averaged across the size dimension.

\begin{verbatim}
R> options_customized <- portfolio_sort_options(

+    exclude_financials = TRUE,
+    exclude_utilities = TRUE,
+    exclude_negative_earnings = TRUE
+  ),
+  breakpoint_options_main = breakpoint_options(
+    n_portfolios = 5,
+    breakpoints_exchanges = "NYSE",
+    breakpoints_min_size_threshold = 0.2
+  ),
+  breakpoint_options_secondary = breakpoint_options(
+    n_portfolios = 2,
+    breakpoints_exchanges = "NYSE"
+  )
+)
\end{verbatim}

\begin{verbatim}
R> portfolio_returns_customized <- implement_portfolio_sort(
+  data = sorting_data,
+  sorting_variables = c("asset_growth", "mktcap_lag"),
+  sorting_method = "bivariate-dependent",
+  rebalancing_month = 7,
+  portfolio_sort_options = options_customized,
+  min_portfolio_size = 10,
+  quiet = TRUE
+)
R> factor_returns_customized <- portfolio_returns_customized |>
+  compute_long_short_returns(direction = "bottom_minus_top")
\end{verbatim}

A single options object (\texttt{options\_customized}) bundles every
methodological choice that distinguishes this specification from the
baseline; nothing in the surrounding pipeline changes. Switching from a
univariate to a size-controlled dependent bivariate sort is a one-word
change in \texttt{sorting\_method}, and the secondary breakpoint object
is supplied alongside the primary one without any additional work.
Setting \texttt{quiet\ =\ TRUE} suppresses the diagnostic messages from
\texttt{filter\_sorting\_data()}, which report how many observations
each filter drops; setting \texttt{quiet\ =\ FALSE} (the default) is
useful when iterating to track sample-construction effects.

\subsubsection{Asset pricing tests}\label{sec-fama-macbeth}

A long-short return is one summary of whether asset growth predicts the
cross-section of returns. A complementary view comes from Fama-MacBeth
regressions \citep{fama.1973}, which estimate the average risk premium
associated with a characteristic after running cross-sectional
regressions period by period. \pkg{tidyfinance} implements the standard
procedure in \texttt{estimate\_fama\_macbeth()}, which takes a panel and
a model formula, fits one cross-sectional ordinary least squares (OLS)
regression per period, and aggregates the period-level coefficients into
average risk premia with Newey-West standard errors with automatic lag
selection.

Here, we winsorize the AG signal at the 1st and 99th percentiles to keep
extreme observations from dominating the cross-sectional regressions and
pair it with log market capitalization as a size control.

+  drop_na(asset_growth, mktcap_lag, ret_excess) |>
+  group_by(date) |>
+  mutate(
+    asset_growth = winsorize(x = asset_growth, cut = 0.01),
+    log_mktcap = log(mktcap_lag)
+  ) |>
+  ungroup()
\end{verbatim}

\begin{verbatim}
R> fm_results <- estimate_fama_macbeth(
+  data = fm_data,
+  model = "ret_excess ~ asset_growth + log_mktcap"
+)

throughout the rest of the paper.

\section{A transparent risk factor library}\label{sec-library}

\subsection{Specification grid}\label{sec-specification-grid}

When every construction choice is configurable, no single combination is
canonical. Rather than ship one default series and force users to
rebuild the pipeline for every variant, we run \pkg{tidyfinance} across
many defensible combinations and publish the results as a precomputed
factor library. The complete construction pipeline is openly available
at
\href{https://github.com/tidy-finance/factor-library}{github.com/tidy-finance/factor-library},
so every step from raw data to published series can be inspected and
reproduced. \citet{walter.2024} and \citet{gruber.2025}, among others,
argue for systematic enumeration over construction choices in preference
to any single canonical specification.

The library is constructed by running each combination of the choices in
Table~\ref{tbl-sorting-decisions} on the same underlying CRSP-Compustat
universe. Beyond the variation Table~\ref{tbl-sorting-decisions} makes
explicit, two sample filters apply across all specifications. An
\emph{earnings filter} drops firms with non-positive income before
extraordinary items (\texttt{ib}), which mitigates the influence of
financial distress on the resulting portfolios. An \emph{age filter}
drops firms younger than two years, which ensures the sorting variable
is based on stable financial information. Both filters are exposed as
parameters of \texttt{download\_data()}; users who want to switch them
off can override the default.

\subsection{Access}\label{sec-library-access}

Researchers retrieve precomputed series without rebuilding the pipeline.
\pkg{tidyfinance} hosts the library on Hugging Face and exposes it
through \texttt{download\_data()}. The library is a versioned downstream
artifact: the manuscript itself consumes it through the same
\texttt{download\_data()} calls, so reproducing this paper requires only
a download rather than a rerun of the construction code. Hugging Face
retains a full commit history of each repository in the same way GitHub
does, so legacy versions of the factor library remain accessible by
referencing earlier snapshots; users can therefore reproduce results
computed against an older release of the library even after the current
version has been updated. An interactive dashboard at
\href{https://factors.tidy-finance.org}{factors.tidy-finance.org}
provides the same series through a browser interface. The companion
\proglang{Python} package \pkg{tidyfinance} offers an identical API for
users who prefer to access the factor library data from
\proglang{Python}.

\subsection{Construction-choice
sensitivity}\label{sec-library-sensitivity}

We illustrate the kind of sensitivity analysis the library supports by
computing a Fama-French three-factor alpha for each specification of the
asset-growth factor in \texttt{ag\_data}. Because the factor return is
the long-short payoff of a self-financing portfolio, it is already in
excess form, and the risk-free rate does not enter the regression. The
Fama-French three factors are available from the earlier
\texttt{download\_data(domain\ =\ "factors\_ff",\ dataset\ =\ "Fama/French\ 3\ Factors")}
call in Section~\ref{sec-data-access}: \texttt{ff\_factors} contains the
monthly market excess return (\texttt{mkt\_excess}), the size factor
(\texttt{smb}), and the value factor (\texttt{hml}). For each
specification \(i\), we estimate \[
r_{i,t} = \alpha_i + \beta_i \, \text{MKT}_t^e + s_i \, \text{SMB}_t + h_i \, \text{HML}_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}
\]

by OLS, where \(r_{i,t}\) is the long-short return of specification
\(i\) in month \(t\). We report the intercept \(\alpha_i\), annualized
by multiplying by 12, as the abnormal return that the three-factor model
leaves unexplained. Figure~\ref{fig-ag-alpha} plots the histogram of
these alphas across all 17,280 specifications.

\begin{verbatim}
R> ag_alpha <- ag_data |>
+  left_join(ff_factors, join_by(date)) |>
+  group_by(id) |>
+  reframe(estimate_model(
+    pick(everything()),
+    model = "ret ~ mkt_excess + hml + smb"
+  )) |>
+  select(id, alpha = intercept) |>
+  mutate(alpha = alpha * 12)
\end{verbatim}

}

The dispersion in Figure~\ref{fig-ag-alpha} shows that construction
choices materially shape the estimated premium. Across the 17,280
specifications, annualized alphas average 4.34\%, with a median of
3.77\%, a cross-specification standard deviation of 2.44\%, and a range
from -0.31\% to 17.67\%. Differences of this magnitude across defensible
defaults echo the non-standard-errors evidence in \citet{menkveld.2024}:
where the breakpoint is placed, when the portfolio rebalances, and which
sample filters apply can move the headline number by more than
conventional sampling uncertainty would suggest.

\section{Summary and discussion}\label{sec-summary}

Empirical asset pricing depends on dozens of construction choices that
no consensus pins down. \pkg{tidyfinance} makes those choices
first-class objects in the workflow: each is an argument, each has a
documented default, each can be varied without rebuilding the pipeline.
Disagreements over construction become empirical questions, not implicit
assumptions buried in code.

We will maintain and extend the package on CRAN. At the time of writing,
all functions are covered by comprehensive unit tests, with code
coverage reported at 100\% by
\href{https://app.codecov.io/gh/tidy-finance/r-tidyfinance}{Codecov},
and each release is accompanied by passing R CMD check results across
multiple operating systems, including the latest macOS, Windows, and
Ubuntu environments. The companion Hugging Face artifacts (the spliced
risk-free rate and the precomputed factor library) are refreshed on a
scheduled GitHub Actions workflow, so the published series track new
WRDS releases without manual intervention.

Companion materials at \href{https://tidy-finance.org}{tidy-finance.org}
integrate \pkg{tidyfinance} into open coursework and self-study tracks
for graduate finance and asset pricing in both \proglang{R} and
\proglang{Python}. The site also hosts a reviewed channel through which
researchers can publish replications and new construction routines, so
the catalog of factors and conventions can grow with the literature
rather than freezing at the package's release.

\section{Variable construction}\label{sec-variable-details}

\subsection{Financing}\label{financing}

\textbf{CDI - Composite Debt Issuance}

Following \citet{lyandres.2008}, we compute composite debt issuance as
the logarithmic growth rate of book debt (current debt, DLC, plus
long-term debt, DLTT) over the last five years:

\[
CDI_t = \log(DLC_{t} + DLTT_{t}) - \log(DLC_{t-60} + DLTT_{t-60})\text{.}
\]

We replace missing values of DLC and DLTT with zero. The sum of DLC and
DLTT must be strictly positive; otherwise, the variable is set to
missing.

\textbf{CSI - Composite Share Issuance}

Following \citet{daniel.2006}, we measure composite share issuance from
CRSP data as the difference between the change in market equity (ME)
(i.e., the shares outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times the absolute value
of their price, \texttt{altprc}, in million USD) and the cumulative log
return (where \(r\) is a simple return) in each month from \(t\) to
\(t-60\), i.e.,

\[
CSI_t = \log(ME_t / ME_{t-60}) - \sum^t_{\tau = t-59}\log(1 + r_{\tau})\text{.}
\]

\textbf{DBE - Change in Common Equity}

Following \citet{richardson.2005}, we calculate the change in common
equity as the change in common equity (CEQ) over one year scaled by
lagged assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
DBE_{t} = \frac{CEQ_{t} - CEQ_{t-12}}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{DCOL - Change in Current Operating Liabilities}

Following \citet{richardson.2005}, we measure the change in current
operating liabilities as the change in current operating liabilities
(i.e., current liabilities, LCT, minus current debt, DLC) over one year
scaled by lagged assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
DCOL_{t} = \frac{(LCT_{t} - DLC_{t}) - (LCT_{t-12} - DLC_{t-12})}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

We set the missing values in DLC to zero.

\textbf{DFNL - Change in Financial Liabilities}

Following \citet{richardson.2005}, we measure the change in financial
liabilities (i.e., the sum of current debt, DLC, plus long-term debt,
DLTT, plus preferred stocks, PSTK) over one year scaled by lagged assets
(AT), i.e.,

\[
DFNL_{t} = \frac{(DLC_{t} + DLTT_{t} + PSTK_{t}) - (DLC_{t-12} + DLTT_{t-12} + PSTK_{t-12})}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

We replace the missing values of DLC, DLTT, and PSTK with zero, if at
least one of them is available.

\textbf{NDF - Net Debt Financing}

Following \citet{bradshaw.2006}, we measure net debt financing as cash
proceeds from the issuance of long-term debt (DLTIS) minus cash payments
for long-term debt reductions (DLTR) plus net changes in current debt
(DLCCH) scaled by the average total assets (AT) over the current and the
last year, i.e.,

\[
NDF_{t} = \frac{DLTIS_{t} - DLTR_{t} + DLCCH_{t}}{\frac{1}{2} \big(AT_{t} + AT_{t-12} \big)}\text{.}
\]

We replace the missing values of DLCCH with zero.

\textbf{NEF - Net Equity Financing}

Following \citet{bradshaw.2006}, we measure net equity financing as
proceeds from the sale of common and preferred stocks (SSTK) minus
payments for the repurchase of common and preferred stocks (PRSTKC)
minus cash payments for dividends (DV) scaled by the average total
assets (AT) over the current and the last year, i.e.,

\[
NEF_{t} = \frac{SSTK_{t} - PRSTKC_{t} - DV_{t}}{\frac{1}{2} \big(AT_{t} + AT_{t-12} \big)}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{NXF - Net External Financing}

Following \citet{bradshaw.2006}, we measure net external financing as
the sum of net debt financing (NDF) and net equity financing (NEF) as
defined above.

\subsection{Intangibles}\label{intangibles}

\textbf{HR - Hiring Rate}

Following \citet{belo.2014}, we measure the hiring rate as the change in
the number of employees (EMP) over one year scaled by the average number
of employees, i.e.,

\[
HR_{t} = \frac{EMP_{t} - EMP_{t-12}}{\frac{1}{2} \big(EMP_{t} + EMP_{t-12} \big)}\text{.}
\]

We exclude firms with a hiring rate of zero.

\textbf{OL - Operating Leverage}

Following \citet{novy-marx.2011}, we measure operating leverage as cost
of goods sold (COGS) plus selling, general, and administrative expenses
(XSGA), both scaled by current total assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
OL_{t} = \frac{COGS_{t} + XSGA_{t}}{AT_{t}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{RER - Real-Estate Ratio}

Following \citet{tuzel.2010}, we measure the real-estate ratio in two
subperiods differently.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\tightlist
\item
  Before 1983, the real-estate ratio is the sum of buildings (PPENB) and
  capital leases (PPENLS) scaled by net property, plant, and equipment
  (PPENT), i.e.,
\end{enumerate}

\[
RER_{t} = \frac{PPENB_{t} + PPENLS_{t}}{PPENT_{t}}\text{.}
\]

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{(\alph{enumi})}
\setcounter{enumi}{1}
\tightlist
\item
  After the end of 1983, the real-estate ratio is the sum of buildings
  at cost (FATB) and leases at cost (FATL), both divided by gross
  property, plant, and equipment (PPEGT), i.e.,
\end{enumerate}

\[
RER_{t} = \frac{FATB_{t} + FATL_{t}}{PPEGT_{t}}\text{.}
\]

Subsequently, we winsorize the real-estate ratios in each fiscal year at
the first and 99\(\text{th}\) percentile. The industry-adjusted
real-estate ratio is obtained by subtracting the industry average
real-estate ratio from each stock's real-estate ratio. We use 2-digit
SIC codes to assign stocks to industries. We always require at least
five observations to calculate the industry average each year.

\subsection{Investment}\label{investment}

\textbf{ACI - Abnormal Corporate Investment}

Following \citet{titman.2004}, we measure abnormal corporate investments
using the variable CE, which is capital expenditures (\texttt{capx})
scaled by sales (\texttt{sale}), then ACI is given by CE scaled by CE
from the previous three years minus one, i.e.,

\[
ACI_{t} = \frac{CE_{t}}{\frac{1}{3} \big(CE_{t-12} + CE_{t-24} + CE_{t-36} \big)}-1\text{.}
\]

We follow \citet{hou.2020} and exclude stocks with sales below 10
million dollars.

\textbf{AG - Asset Growth}

Following \citet{cooper.2008}, we measure asset growth as the change in
total assets (AT) over one year scaled by lagged assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
AG_{t} = \frac{AT_{t} - AT_{t-12}}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{DINV - Inventory Changes}

Following \citet{thomas.2002}, we measure the change in inventory as the
annual change in inventories (INVT) scaled by average total assets (AT)
over the current and the last year, i.e.,

\[
DINV_{t} = \frac{INVT_{t} - INVT_{t-12}}{\frac{1}{2} \big(AT_{t} + AT_{t-12} \big)}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{DNOA - Change in Net Operating Assets}

Following \citet{hirshleifer.2004}, we measure net operating assets
(defined as \(NOA^{int}\)) as

\[
NOA^{int}_{t} = (AT_{t} - CHE_{t}) - (AT_{t} - DLC_{t} - DLTT_{t} - MIB_{t} - PSTK_{t} - CEQ_{t})\text{,}
\]

where AT corresponds to total assets, CHE to cash and short-term
investments, DLC to current debt, DLTT to long-term debt, MIB to
minority interests, PSTK to the value of preferred stocks, and CEQ to
common equity. Missing values in DLC, DLTT, MIB, and PSTK are set to
zero. Then, the change in net operating assets is the difference between
net operating assets of fiscal year \(t\) and fiscal year \(t-12\)
scaled by lagged assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
DNOA_{t} = \frac{NOA^{int}_{t} - NOA^{int}_{t-12}}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{DPIA - Change in Property, Plant, Equipment, and Inventory}

Following \citet{lyandres.2008}, we measure the change in property,
plant, equipment, and inventory by combining the change in gross
property, plant, and equipment (PPEGT) with the annual change in
inventory (INVT) and scaling this sum by lagged total assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
DPIA_{t} = \frac{(PPEGT_{t} - PPEGT_{t-12}) + (INVT_{t} - INVT_{t-12})}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{DWC - Change in Net Non-Cash Working Capital}

Following \citet{richardson.2005}, we measure non-cash working capital
as

\[
WC_{t} = (ACT_{t} - CHE_{t}) - (LCT_{t} - DLC_{t})\text{,}
\]

where ACT corresponds to current assets, CHE to cash, LCT to current
liabilities, and DLC to short-term debt. We set the missing values in
DLC to zero. Then, the change in net non-cash working capital
corresponds to the change in \(WC\) from fiscal year \(t-12\) to fiscal
year \(t\) scaled by lagged total assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
DWC_{t} = \frac{WC_{t} - WC_{t-12}}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{IG - Investment Growth}

Following \citet{xing.2008}, we measure investment growth as the annual
change in capital expenditures (CAPX) scaled by lagged capital
expenditures, i.e.,

\[
IG_{t} = \frac{CAPX_{t} - CAPX_{t-12}}{CAPX_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{NOA - Net Operating Assets}

Following \citet{hirshleifer.2004}, we measure net operating assets
(defined as \(NOA^{int}\)) as

\[
NOA^{int}_{t} = (AT_{t} - CHE_{t}) - (AT_{t} - DLC_{t} - DLTT_{t} - MIB_{t} - PSTK_{t} - CEQ_{t})\text{,}
\]

where AT corresponds to total assets, CHE to cash and short-term
investments, DLC to current debt, DLTT to long-term debt, MIB to
minority interests, PSTK to the value of preferred stocks, and CEQ to
common equity. Missing values in DLC, DLTT, MIB, and PSTK are set to
zero. Then, net operating assets are the net operating assets of fiscal
year \(t\) scaled by lagged assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
NOA_{t} = \frac{NOA^{int}_{t}}{AT_{t-12}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{OA - Operating Accruals}

Following \citet{sloan.1996} (before 1988) and \citet{hribar.2002}
(after 1988), we measure operating accruals until 1988 as

\[
OA_{t} = \frac{(\Delta ACT_{t} - \Delta CHE_{t}) - (\Delta LCT_{t} - \Delta DLC_{t} - \Delta TXP_{t}) -DP_{t}}{AT_{t-12}}\text{,}
\]

where ACT is current assets, CHE cash, LCT current liabilities, DLC
short-term debt, TXP taxes payable, and DP depreciation and
amortization. Moreover, we replace the missing values in DLC and TXP
with zero. \(\Delta\) represents changes over one year. From 1988
onwards, we measure operating accruals as

\[
OA_{t} = \frac{NI_{t} - OANCF_{t}}{AT_{t-12}}\text{,}
\]

where NI is net income and OANCF corresponds to net cash flow from
operations.

\subsection{Momentum}\label{momentum}

\textbf{MOM - Return Momentum}

Following \citet{fama.1996}, we measure return momentum from monthly
CRSP returns (where \(r\) is a simple return) as the cumulative return
from month \(t-11\) to month \(t-1\), skipping the most recent month,
i.e.,

\[
MOM_t = \exp\left(\sum^{t-1}_{\tau = t-11}\log(1 + r_{\tau})\right) - 1\text{.}
\]

\textbf{RMOM - Residual Momentum}

Following \citet{blitz.2011}, we define the 11-month residual momentum
(RMOM) in each month and for each stock as cumulative residual returns
from month \(t-11\) to month \(t-1\), scaled by the standard deviation
of residual returns over the same time horizon. Residual returns
\(\epsilon\) are obtained in each month from regressing monthly simple,
excess stock returns from month \(t-1\) to month \(t-36\) on the
Fama-French three-factor model. Throughout these rolling regressions, we
always require 36 monthly returns.

\textbf{RS - Revenue Surprise}

Following \citet{jegadeesh.2006}, we measure the change in revenues per
share (DRPS) from quarterly Compustat data as quarterly sales (SALEQ)
scaled by the product of quarterly shares outstanding (CSHPRQ) times
their cumulative adjustment factor (AJEXQ), i.e.,

\[
DRPS_{q}= \frac{SALEQ_{q}}{CSHPRQ_{q} \cdot AJEXQ_{q}} - \frac{SALEQ_{q-4}}{CSHPRQ_{q-4} \cdot AJEXQ_{q-4}}\text{.}
\]

Then, the revenue surprise corresponds to the change in revenues per
share over the last four quarters scaled by the standard deviation of
the change in revenues per share over the last eight quarters. We
require at least six quarterly observations for this rolling standard
deviation and remove infinite values. Finally, the earnings announcement
date has to be after the fiscal quarter end date.\footnote{We follow the
  convention of \citet{hou.2020} and do not estimate a random walk model
  with drift to compute the surprise.}

\textbf{SUE - Standardized Unexpected Earnings}

Following \citet{foster.1984}, we measure unexpected earnings per share
(UES) from quarterly Compustat data as the change in split-adjusted
earnings (which are equal to quarterly earnings per share, EPSPXQ,
scaled by the correction factor for shares outstanding, AJEXQ) per share
from its value four quarters ago, i.e.,

\[
UES_{q} = \frac{EPSPXQ_{q}}{AJEXQ_{q}} - \frac{EPSPXQ_{q-4}}{AJEXQ_{q-4}}\text{.}
\]

Then, standardized unexpected earnings are defined as unexpected
earnings per share divided by the standard deviation of unexpected
earnings per share over the previous eight quarters. We require at least
six quarterly observations for this rolling standard deviation. Finally,
the earnings announcement date has to be after the fiscal quarter end
date.\footnote{We follow the convention of \citet{hou.2020} and do not
  estimate a random walk model with drift to compute the surprise.}

\textbf{52W - 52-Week High}

Following \citet{george.2004}, we measure the 52-week high as month
\(t\)'s closing split-adjusted stock price scaled by the highest daily
split-adjusted stock price over the previous twelve months. We only
consider stock-month pairs with more than three valid price
observations.

\textbf{REV - Long-Term Reversal}

Following \citet{debondt.1985}, we measure the long-term reversal effect
by the cumulative returns from month \(t-60\) to month \(t-13\), i.e.,

\[
REV_t = \exp\left(\sum^{t-13}_{\tau = t-60}\log(1 + r_{\tau})\right) - 1\text{.}
\]

\subsection{Profitability}\label{profitability}

\textbf{CBOP - Cash-Based Operating Profitability}

Following \citet{ball.2016}, we measure cash-based operating
profitability as

\begin{align*}
CBOP_{t} = &\big(REVT_{t} - COGS_{t} - XSGA_{t} + XRD_{t} \\
&-\Delta RECT_{t} - \Delta INVT_{t} - \Delta XPP_{t} + \Delta DRC_{t} \\
&+ \Delta DRLT_{t} + \Delta AP_{t} + \Delta XACC_{t} \big) / AT_{t}\text{,}
\end{align*}

where REVT is total revenue, COGS are cost of goods sold, XSGA are
selling, general, and administrative expenses, and XRD are R\&D
expenses. Moreover, \(\Delta\) refers to one-year changes, which we
measure for accounts receivable (RECT), inventory (INVT), prepaid
expenses (XPP), deferred revenues (the sum of current, DRC, and
long-term, DRLT), trade accounts payable (AP), and accrued expenses
(XACC). We set the missing values of XRD and all missing changes to
zero.

\textbf{GPA - Gross Profits to Assets}

Following \citet{novy-marx.2013}, we measure gross profits to assets as
total revenues (REVT) minus cost of goods sold (COGS) scaled by current
total assets (AT), i.e.,

\[
GPA_{t} = \frac{REVT_{t} - COGS_{t}}{AT_{t}}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{O - Ohlson's O-score}

Following \citet{ohlson.1980}, we measure the O-score with the following
linear relation:

\begin{align*}
O_{t} = &- 1.32 - 0.407 \cdot \log(AT_{t}) + 6.03 \cdot \frac{DLC_{t} + DLTT_{t}}{AT_{t}} \\
&- 1.43 \cdot \frac{ACT_{t} - LCT_{t}}{AT_{t}} + 0.076 \cdot \frac{LCT_{t}}{AT_{t}} \\
&- 1.72 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{LT_{t} > AT_{t}} - 2.37 \cdot \frac{NI_{t}}{AT_{t}} - 1.83 \cdot \frac{PI_{t} + DP_{t}}{LT_{t}} \\
&+ 0.285 \cdot \mathbf{1}_{NI_{t} < 0 \text{ and } NI_{t-12} < 0} - 0.521 \cdot \frac{NI_{t}-NI_{t-12}}{|NI_{t}| + |NI_{t-12}|}\text{,}
\end{align*}

where AT corresponds to total assets, DLC to short-term debt, DLTT to
long-term debt, ACT to current assets, LCT to current liabilities, LT to
total liabilities, PI to pretax income, DP to depreciation and
amortization, and NI to net income. We winsorize all variables except
for dummy variables at the first and 99\(\text{th}\) percentile of their
respective distribution.

\textbf{OPE - Operating Profits to Book Equity}

Following \citet{fama.2015}, we measure operating profits to book equity
as

\[
OPE_{t} = \frac{REVT_{t} - COGS_{t} - XSGA_{t} - XINT_{t}}{BE_{t}}\text{,}
\]

where REVT corresponds to total revenues, COGS to cost of goods sold,
XSGA to selling, general and administrative expenses, XINT to interest
expenses, and BE to book equity. The definition of book equity is (a)
the book equity of shareholders plus (b) deferred taxes and investment
tax credit (Compustat item \texttt{txditc} or \texttt{txdb} +
\texttt{itcb} if \texttt{txditc} is unavailable) minus (c) the book
value of preferred stock. Regarding (a), the book equity of shareholders
is shareholders' equity (\texttt{seq}), or the sum of common equity
(\texttt{ceq}) and the par value of preferred stock (\texttt{pstk}), or,
if all previous items are unavailable, total assets (\texttt{at}) minus
total liabilities (\texttt{lt}). Regarding (b), deferred taxes
(\texttt{txditc}) or investment tax credits (\texttt{txdb} plus
\texttt{itcb}) if deferred taxes are unavailable. Regarding (c), the
book value of preferred stock corresponds in the following order either
to the redemption value (\texttt{pstkrv}), or the liquidation value
(\texttt{pstkl}), or if all previous items are unavailable to the par
value (\texttt{pstk}). We replace the missing values in items (b) and
(c) with zero. Moreover, missing values in COGS, XSGA, and XINT are set
to zero.

\textbf{ROA - Return on Assets}

Following \citet{balakrishnan.2010}, we measure return on assets as
quarterly income before extraordinary items (IBQ) relative to lagged
quarterly total assets (ATQ), i.e.,

\[
ROA_{q} = \frac{IBQ_{q}}{ATQ_{q-1}}\text{.}
\]

Finally, the earnings announcement date must be after the fiscal
quarter-end date.

\textbf{ROE - Return on Equity}

Following \citet{hou.2014}, we measure return on equity as quarterly
income before extraordinary items (IBQ) relative to lagged book equity
(BEQ)

\[
ROE_{q} = \frac{IBQ_{q}}{BEQ_{q-1}}\text{.}
\]

Quarterly book equity (BEQ) is computed as the (a) book equity of
shareholders plus (b) balance sheet deferred taxes and investment tax
credit minus (c) the book value of preferred stock. Regarding (a), we
measure book equity of shareholders by shareholders' equity
(\texttt{seqq}), or the sum of common equity (\texttt{ceqq}) and the par
value of preferred stock (\texttt{pstkq}), or if all previous items are
unavailable by total assets (\texttt{atq}) minus total liabilities
(\texttt{ltq}). Regarding (b), the balance sheet deferred taxes and
investment tax credit is \texttt{txditcq}, or \texttt{txdbq}, or zero if
both are missing. Regarding (c), the book value of preferred stocks
corresponds to \texttt{pstkrq}, or \texttt{pstkq}, or zero if both are
unavailable. Finally, the earnings announcement date must be after the
fiscal quarter-end date.

\textbf{TBI - Taxable Income to Book Income}

Following \citet{lev.2004}, we measure taxable income to book income as
pretax income (PI) scaled by net income (NI)

\[
TBI_{t} = \frac{PI_{t}}{NI_{t}}\text{.}
\]

We require positive pretax and net income.

\subsection{Size}\label{size}

\textbf{ME - Size}

Following \citet{fama.1992}, we measure size as the natural logarithm of
the market equity. We obtain market equity data from CRSP by multiplying
the shares outstanding (SHROUT \(\times\) 1,000, as they are given in
thousands) with the corresponding share price (the absolute value of
ALTPRC), i.e.,

\[
ME_{t} = \log\left(SHROUT_{t} \times 1000 \times |\text{ALTPRC}_{t}|\right)\text{.}
\]

\subsection{Trading frictions}\label{trading-frictions}

\textbf{AMI - Amihud Illiquidity Measure}

Following \citet{amihud.2002}, we measure the illiquidity from daily
return-to-volume (RV), which is the absolute daily return (r) scaled by
the daily dollar trading volume (product of VOL and PRC), i.e.,

\[
RV_{t} = \frac{|r_{d}|}{PRC_{t} \times VOL_{t}}\text{.}
\]

Then, the Amihud illiquidity measure corresponds to the average
return-to-volume estimate over the last six months. We require at least
50 observations for this average and adjust the trading volume of NASDAQ
stocks according to \citet{gao.2010}.

\textbf{BETA - Beta Relative to the Market}

Following \citet{fama.1973}, we measure the market beta \(\beta_{1}\)
from monthly CRSP data. Specifically, we run the following time series
regression over the previous five years:

\[
r_{t}^{e} = \alpha + \beta_{1} \cdot (\text{MKT}_{t} - R_{t}^{f}) + u_{t}\text{.}
\]

Moreover, we require at least 24 monthly observations for the regression
above.

\textbf{DTV - Dollar Trading Volume}

Following \citet{brennan.1998}, we measure dollar trading volume from
daily CRSP data as the average dollar trading volume (product of
\texttt{vol} and \texttt{prc}) from month \(t-6\) to month \(t-1\) (with
at least 50 observations). Moreover, we adjust dollar trading volume
from NASDAQ according to \citet{gao.2010}.

\textbf{ISKEW - Idiosyncratic Skewness Relative to the Fama-French
three-factor model}

Following \citet{bali.2016}, we regress daily excess returns in each
month on the Fama-French three-factor model (with a minimum of 15 daily
observations), i.e.,

\[
r_{t}^{e} = \alpha + \beta_{1} \cdot (\text{MKT}_{t} - R_{t}^{f}) + \beta_{2} \cdot \text{SMB}_{t} + \beta_{3} \cdot \text{HML}_{t} + u_{t}\text{.}
\]

Then, idiosyncratic skewness is measured as the skewness of residuals
\(u_{t}\).

\textbf{IVOL - Idiosyncratic Volatility Relative to the Fama-French
model}

Following \citet{ang.2006}, we compute idiosyncratic volatility relative
to the Fama-French three-factor model as the volatility of residuals
from the following regression (with a minimum of 15 daily observations)

\[
r_{t}^{e} = \alpha + \beta_{1} \cdot (\text{MKT}_{t} - R_{t}^{f}) + \beta_{2} \cdot \text{SMB}_{t} + \beta_{3} \cdot \text{HML}_{t} + u_{t}\text{.}
\]

\textbf{MDR - Maximum Daily Return}

Following \citet{bali.2011}, we measure the maximum daily (excess)
return in each month from daily CRSP data (with at least 15 return
observations).

\textbf{TUR - Share Turnover}

Following \citet{datar.1998}, we measure the daily share turnover of
each stock as the number of shares traded (VOL) scaled by the number of
shares outstanding (SHROUT), i.e.,

\[
T_{t} = \frac{VOL}{SHROUT}\text{.}
\]

Then, the share turnover for each firm in each month \(t\) is the
average daily share turnover over the previous six months (with at least
50 observations). Moreover, we adjust the trading volume of NASDAQ
stocks according to \citet{gao.2010}.

\subsection{Valuation}\label{valuation}

\textbf{BM - Book Equity to Market Equity}

Following \citet{davis.2000}, we measure the book-to-market ratio as
book equity (BE) from Compustat divided by market equity (ME) from CRSP
(i.e., the fiscal-year end's shares outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times
the absolute value of their price, \texttt{altprc}, in million USD),
i.e.,

\[
BM_{t} = \frac{BE_{t}}{ME_{t}}\text{.}
\]

The definition of book equity is (a) the book equity of shareholders
plus (b) deferred taxes and investment tax credit (Compustat item
\texttt{txditc} or \texttt{txdb} + \texttt{itcb} if \texttt{txditc} is
unavailable) minus (c) the book value of preferred stock. Regarding (a),
the book equity of shareholders is shareholders' equity (\texttt{seq}),
or the sum of common equity (\texttt{ceq}) and the par value of
preferred stock (\texttt{pstk}), or, if all previous items are
unavailable, total assets (\texttt{at}) minus total liabilities
(\texttt{lt}). Regarding (b), deferred taxes (\texttt{txditc}) or
investment tax credits (\texttt{txdb} plus \texttt{itcb}) if deferred
taxes are unavailable. Regarding (c), the book value of preferred stock
corresponds in the following order either to the redemption value
(\texttt{pstkrv}), or the liquidation value (\texttt{pstkl}), or if all
previous items are unavailable to the par value (\texttt{pstk}). We
replace the missing values in items (b) and (c) with zero.

\textbf{CFM - Cash Flow to Market Equity}

Following \citet{lakonishok.1994}, we measure the cash-flow-to-price
ratio as income before extraordinary items (IB) plus depreciation (DP)
scaled by market equity from CRSP (i.e., the fiscal-year end's shares
outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times the absolute value of their price,
\texttt{altprc}, in million USD), i.e.,

\[
CFM_{t} = \frac{IB_{t} + DP_{t}}{ME_{t}}\text{.}
\]

We exclude all stocks with negative cash flows.

\textbf{DM - Debt to Market Equity}

Following \citet{bhandari.1988}, we measure the debt-to-market ratio as
short-term debt (DLC) plus long-term debt (DLTT) from Compustat divided
by market equity from CRSP (i.e., the fiscal-year end's shares
outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times the absolute value of their price,
\texttt{altprc}, in million USD), i.e.,

\[
DM_{t} = \frac{DLC_{t} + DLTT_{t}}{ME_{t}}\text{.}
\]

We exclude stocks with missing DLC and DLTT observations.

\textbf{EBM - Enterprise Book Equity to Market Equity}

Following \citet{penman.2007}, we measure enterprise book equity scaled
by market equity as net debt plus book equity scaled by net debt plus
market equity, i.e.,

\[
EBM_{t} = \frac{(DLTT_{t} + DLC_{t} + PSTK_{t} + DVPA_{t} - TSTKP_{t}) - CHE_{t} + BE_{t}}{(DLTT_{t} + DLC_{t} + PSTK_{t} + DVPA_{t} - TSTKP_{t}) - CHE_{t} + ME_{t}}\text{,}
\]

where DLTT corresponds to long-term debt, DLC to current debt, PSTK to
the value of preferred stock, DVPA to preferred dividends in arrears,
TSTKP to preferred treasury stock, CHE to cash and short-term
investments, and ME to the market equity from CRSP (i.e., the
fiscal-year end's shares outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times the
absolute value of their price, \texttt{altprc}, in million USD). Lastly,
in this case, book equity BE is computed as common equity (\texttt{ceq})
plus TSTKP minus DVPA. Missing observations in DVPA and TSTKP are set to
zero. Additionally, we require that the sum of net debt and book equity,
as well as the sum of net debt plus market equity, is positive.

\textbf{EM - Earnings to Market Equity}

Following \citet{basu.1983}, we measure the earnings-to-price ratio as
income before extraordinary items (IB) from Compustat divided by market
equity from CRSP (i.e., the fiscal-year end's shares outstanding,
\texttt{shrout}, times the absolute value of their price,
\texttt{altprc}, in million USD), i.e.,

\[
EM_{t} = \frac{IB_{t}}{ME_{t}}\text{.}
\]

We exclude firms with negative earnings.

\textbf{NDM - Net Debt to Market Equity}

Following \citet{penman.2007}, we measure net debt to price as net debt
from Compustat scaled by market equity from CRSP (i.e., the fiscal-year
end's shares outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times the absolute value of
their price, \texttt{altprc}, in million USD), i.e.,

\[
NDM_{t} = \frac{(DLTT_{t} + DLC_{t} + PSTK_{t} + DVPA_{t} - TSTKP_{t}) - CHE_{t}}{ME_{t}}\text{,}
\]

where DLTT corresponds to long-term debt, DLC to current debt, PSTK to
the value of preferred stock, DVPA to preferred dividends in arrears,
TSTKP to preferred treasury stock, and CHE to cash and short-term
investments. Lastly, missing observations in DVPA and TSTKP are set to
zero.

\textbf{NPY - Net Payout Yield}

Following \citet{boudoukh.2007}, we measure the net payout yield as net
payouts from Compustat scaled by market equity from CRSP (i.e., the
fiscal-year end's shares outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times the
absolute value of their price, \texttt{altprc}, in million USD), i.e.,

\begin{align*}
NPY_{t} = &\big[(DVC_{t} + PRSTKC_{t} + \Delta PSTKRV_{t} \cdot {1}_{\Delta PSTKRV <0}) \\
&- (SSTK_{t} - \Delta PSTKRV_{t} \cdot {1}_{\Delta PSTKRV >0})\big] / ME_{t}\text{,}
\end{align*}

where DVC are dividends from common stock, PRSTKC is the purchase of
common and preferred stock, PSTKRV is the value of the net number of
preferred stocks outstanding, and SSTK reflects the sale of common and
preferred stocks. \({1}_{\Delta PSTKRV <0}\) is a dummy variable that is
one if the annual change in PSTKRV is negative and zero otherwise.
Moreover, we exclude stocks with negative net payouts.

\textbf{OCM - Operating Cash Flow to Market Equity}

Following \citet{desai.2004}, we measure the ratio of operating cash
flows to price as operating cash flows (OC) from Compustat divided by
the market equity from CRSP (i.e., the fiscal-year end's shares
outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times the absolute value of their price,
\texttt{altprc}, in million USD), i.e.,

\[
OCM_{t} = \frac{OC_{t}}{ME_{t}}\text{.}
\]

Before 1988, we measure OC as funds from operations (\texttt{fopt})
minus the change in working capital (item \texttt{wcap}). Thereafter, we
measure OC as net cash flows from operating activities (\texttt{oancf}).
Moreover, we exclude firms with negative operating cash flows.

\textbf{SM - Sales to Market Equity}

Following \citet{barbee.1996}, we measure the sales-to-price ratio (SM)
as sales (SALE) from Compustat divided by the market equity from CRSP
(i.e., the fiscal-year end's shares outstanding, \texttt{shrout}, times
the absolute value of their price, \texttt{altprc}, in million USD),
i.e.,

\[
SM_{t} = \frac{SALE_{t}}{ME_{t}}\text{.}
\]
